--- EXAMPLE INPUT ---
Name: Grinder (Block), Owner: Coffee Machine.
Context: receives "whole beans" from Bean Hopper; sends "ground coffee" to

--- EXAMPLE OUTPUT ---
### 1. Role in the real system
The Grinder turns whole beans into ground coffee immediately before brewing. It
sits between the bean hopper, which stores the beans, and the brewing unit, which
forces hot water through the grounds, so it determines the consistency that
drives extraction and, ultimately, how the coffee tastes.

### 2. Hidden and non-obvious links (with real consequences)
The grinder shares a power supply with the water heater. This is invisible in the
coffee flow, but if both draw current at once the machine can exceed its power
budget and deliver a half-warm, weak cup with no obvious broken part. If the
grinder jams, water still heats and pumps still run, yet no grounds reach the
brewing unit, so hot water passes straight through and the drink tastes of
nothing. If the grind is set too coarse, extraction drops and the coffee comes out
weak — a fault that shows up in the cup, two steps downstream, not at the grinder.

### 3. Impact and understanding
Everything downstream of the grinder — the brewing unit and the finished cup —
depends on it delivering usable grounds. Upstream it depends on a filled hopper
and on the shared power supply. A change to grind size, timing or power draw
degrades brew quality even though the brewing unit itself is untouched.

### 4. Issues worth raising
The shared power supply is a real coupling that is easy to overlook and can cause
intermittent under-performance that is hard to trace back to its cause.

### 5. Recommendation
Model the shared power supply as an explicit allocation or constraint so the
grinding/heating coupling is visible and the combined load can be checked against
the machine's power budget.
